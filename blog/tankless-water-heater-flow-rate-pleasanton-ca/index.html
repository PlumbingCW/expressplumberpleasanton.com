Tankless Water Heater Flow Rate in Pleasanton, CA, Explained

A tankless heater is rated in gallons per minute, but the number on the box assumes a certain temperature rise. Here is how to read the rating and match it to your home.

Call (510) 544-1962
By the Pleasanton Express Plumbing teamUpdated November 13, 20268 min read

Flow rate is how much hot water a tankless heater can deliver at once, measured in gallons per minute. The number on a brochure is usually the best case, with warm incoming water and a modest temperature rise. In winter, with colder water, the same unit delivers less.

To size a unit correctly you need two numbers: the flow you want at the fixtures and the temperature rise your water needs. Then you compare them with the capacity of the unit at that rise, not the headline figure.

Flow rate and temperature rise

Temperature rise is the difference between the temperature of the incoming water and the temperature you set. If the incoming water is 60 degrees Fahrenheit and you set 120, the rise is 60 degrees. The heater can only add so much heat per minute, so a bigger rise means a lower flow.

A gas unit rated at 199,000 Btu per hour that is about 80 percent efficient delivers roughly 160,000 Btu per hour to the water. Each gallon per minute needs about 500 Btu per hour for every degree of rise. That gives roughly 9 gallons per minute at a 35 degree rise and roughly 4.5 gallons per minute at a 70 degree rise.

Adding up your fixtures

Make a list of what might run at once. The City of Pleasanton's bathroom handout lists a showerhead at 1.8 gallons per minute at 80 psi, a bathroom faucet at 1.2 gallons per minute and a kitchen faucet at 1.8. Dishwashers and washing machines vary, so read their labels.

Two showers and a running kitchen faucet add up to about 5.4 gallons per minute of mixed water, though the hot portion is less because cold water is blended in at the valve. If your peak demand is higher than the unit can deliver at your rise, you need a larger unit, two units or a different plan.

A simple sizing worksheet

  • List every hot water fixture that may run at the same time.
  • Add their gallons per minute to get peak flow.
  • Estimate your coldest incoming water temperature and choose a set point, often 120 degrees.
  • Subtract to get the rise, then find the unit's capacity at that rise.
  • Pick a unit whose capacity at that rise is higher than your peak flow.

Pleasanton water and what it does to a heat exchanger

The City of Pleasanton's annual water quality report says its water is disinfected with chloramine. It also says hardness runs from 100 mg/L to 450 mg/L, or 6 to 26 grains per gallon, depending on the source of the water. Zone 7 treated surface water averages 86 mg/L, while its groundwater averages 309 mg/L.

Hard water leaves scale on the inside of a heat exchanger, and scale narrows the passages and cuts flow over time. If your flow is falling year over year, scale is a prime suspect, and a flush often brings it back.

Minimum flow, the other number that matters

Every unit has a minimum flow, often around half a gallon per minute, below which the burner will not fire. If you open a tap just a little, the heater may not start, and the water stays cold. That explains why a trickle at a bathroom sink sometimes gives only cold water.

A recirculation loop or a small buffer tank can help in these cases. Our tankless water heater page covers flushing, error codes and the choice between a loop and a buffer tank.

When tankless is not the right choice

A tankless unit is a strong choice for steady, long loads and for a house that wants more space. It is a weaker choice for a very large household with many fixtures, or a house with a gas line that cannot be upsized. In those cases, a larger tank or a heat pump heater may be the better fit.

A worked example for a four-person household

Take a family with two bathrooms that often run two showers at once while someone loads the dishwasher. Two showers at 1.8 gallons per minute add up to 3.6, and the dishwasher adds a modest amount. If the coldest incoming water is about 60 degrees and the set point is 120, the unit must deliver that flow at a 60 degree rise.

A unit that delivers about 5.3 gallons per minute at that rise covers it with a little margin. A smaller unit might manage one shower at a time and struggle with two. That is the kind of mismatch that a sizing worksheet catches before you buy.
